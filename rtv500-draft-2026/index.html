One yardstick Every number on this page counts points above a replaceable starter at that position — value over replacement, which means the same thing whichever position it comes from, so a starter and a reserve can be compared directly. It used to count points above the best player actually left undrafted. That baseline sat at a different level for every position depending on how deeply this draft cleared it — Jared Goff at QB, Samaje Perine at RB, Rashid Shaheed at WR, Juwan Johnson at TE, Minnesota Vikings at D/ST, Will Reichard at K — which handed a running back points it did not hand a quarterback, for reasons that had nothing to do with either of them.
Starters All nine slots of the best lineup that could actually be fielded, kicker and defense included, each floored at zero: a starter worth less than a free agent is worth nothing, not something negative, because you would drop him and sign the free agent. A player who cannot play is not eligible for a slot at all and falls to the bench. Kickers and defenses are valued on projected points rather than value over replacement, which nobody computes for them; the difference from the best one left undrafted is on the same scale either way.
Bench The reserves, summed, floored at zero — a reserve no better than a free agent is worth nothing, since you would simply drop him.
Total Starters plus bench: the whole roster's value over free agents.
Grade Weighted average of all sixteen pick grades, each counting for how much value was on the board when it was made. The only column not partly a function of draft slot — and the one to read least literally: it carries an error of about ±0.21, which is as large as the gap between first and last, so treat the middle of this table as a tie.

How the grades work

Every pick is weighed by what was at stake. The one overall pick had 187 points of value on the table; the last pick had 106.

Three boards, because they disagree in useful ways. Market ADP is a blend weighting FFC highest, since it comes from real ten-team PPR drafts. ECR is FantasyPros alone. VORP is projected points above a replacement starter. Where ADP and ECR sit more than forty places apart, the pessimistic reading is used.

The gap is measured in standard deviations, not places. Real drafts agree to within about three picks on a first-rounder and disagree by nearly twenty on a fifteenth, so a fixed “twenty picks early” is a seven-sigma call in round one and inside the noise in round fifteen. Dividing by each player’s own spread puts every round on the same scale.

Roster need plays no part. Filling a starting slot is what a draft is for, not a decision worth credit; and taking depth while a slot sits open is often correct, because a fourth receiver really can beat the best quarterback left. The one exception is hoarding: past a second quarterback or tight end, or a sixth back or receiver, a pick costs a grade.

Taking the best player left is its own credit. Early on there is no value to capture, so a pick that takes the top player on the board floors at A. That floor is suspended when the sources split, because it is computed from VORP, and VORP has no concept of a player being unable to play.

Bench counts what beats a replaceable starter. A reserve below that line is worth zero, not something negative, because you would drop him. This used to be measured against the best player genuinely left undrafted, which sounds like the more honest question and quietly was not: across this league roughly half of every team’s total came from those positional offsets rather than from the players, and the share swung by twelve points between teams on roster composition alone.

Draft slot is not skill. Slots 1–3 averaged 678 points of starting lineup; slots 8–10 averaged 625. The grade controls for that; the starters column does not.

The ten drafts are closer than the grades look. A team GPA averages sixteen noisy numbers and carries an error of about ±0.21 — the same size as the spread between first and last. Shuffling all 160 picks at random between the teams reproduces a spread this wide about two times in five. The ranking is real; the distances inside it mostly are not.

Grades measure decision quality against what was knowable on the night. Projection R² is about 0.24, so outcome noise is roughly twice the spread of the projections. None of this predicts the season.
